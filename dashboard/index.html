Where the evidence points

Ordered by the strength of what the data supports — not by ease of implementation.

  1. Treat on-time delivery as the primary satisfaction lever
    The cliff effect and the model's ~76–78% weight on delivery timing both say this should outrank most other satisfaction work.
  2. Fix the seller-geography imbalance structurally
    São Paulo sellers fulfilling ~71% of orders is a network-design problem — regional fulfillment or logistics partnerships, not asking individual sellers to try harder.
  3. Give bulky, underperforming categories a dedicated look
    Office furniture and similar categories underperform without an obvious delivery cause — read the review text to confirm whether it's damage in transit or something else.
  4. Deprioritize payment-experience changes
    Installments and payment type barely move review scores once delivery timing is accounted for — effort is better spent elsewhere.
  5. Build a retention program — after delivery is fixed
    Repeat customers are worth roughly double the lifetime spend, but a customer whose first delivery was late won't respond well to a retention nudge.

METHODOLOGY: eight source tables cleaned and joined to one row per order (99,441 rows). Reviews deduplicated to one per order; missing product categories recoded to "unknown" rather than dropped. Delivery-timing analysis scoped to delivered orders with a non-null delivery date.

All figures recomputed directly from the source CSVs in this repository, following the methodology in the accompanying Colab notebook — not read from a fixed export.

SCOPE: order_value = price + freight at the item level, not Olist's margin. This is observational data — associations, not proven causation.

Data: Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, via Kaggle, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Company and partner names in review text were replaced with fictional placeholders by the original publisher.
